## Abstract

While average trends in education have historically improved, social disparities in children's academic skills and educational attainment are persisting in Germany. Both genetic and environmental factors contribute to shaping educational trajectories, yet the culturally specific mechanisms underlying these influences remain poorly understood. Germany offers a unique context to explore gene–environment interactions in education, given its significant historical, social, and political transformations over the past century, including the division and reunification of the country. This study aims to provide insights into whether and when genetic effects on education have remained stable, attenuated, or amplified over historical and social transformations in Germany. We outline an analysis plan probing whether the association of DNA-based biomarkers with (a) educational attainment and (b) relative intergenerational mobility varies by age cohort, region (East/West), and gender. We harmonize and synthesize genomic, educational, and regional data from four German cohorts, encompassing over 15,000 individuals born between 1920 and 2000. Our analysis will offer insights into whether genetic influences on education have shifted over time and differ among social groups, potentially contributing to increasing social disparities in educational attainment and intergenerational mobility.

**Keywords:** Gene–environment interactions, Education, German Reunification, Educational Disparities

## Introduction

While average trends in education have historically improved, social disparities in children's academic skills and educational attainment persist, and may be growing in some metrics in Germany and several other European countries (European Commission, 2024; Maaz & Lörz, 2024; Skopek & Passaretta, 2021). Both genetic and environmental factors contribute to shaping educational trajectories, yet the interplay between the individual and culturally specific mechanisms underlying these influences remains poorly understood. Germany offers a unique context to explore gene–environment interactions in education, given its significant historical and social transformations over the past century, including the division of the country into a state-socialist dictatorship in East Germany and a capitalist democracy in West Germany, and its subsequent reunification.

> **Figure 1.** Our preliminary findings in 1,902 SOEP-G participants showing that polygenic associations with education grew stronger in East (red) compared to West (blue) Germany after reunification (Fraemke et al., 2025, *Psych Sci*).

Large-scale genomic studies have generated DNA-based biomarkers to study the genetics of educational traits. Polygenic indices (PGIs) are summary measures of genetic variants previously found to be associated with, for example, educational attainment in over 3 million people (PGI-Education; Okbay et al., 2022). Figure 1 depicts our previous finding based on 1,902 individuals from the SOEP-G cohort: the predictive power of this PGI-Education did not differ between East and West Germany before reunification but increased in East Germany thereafter (i.e., for those born in 1975 and later; Fraemke et al., 2025). The observed amplification of education-genetic associations aligns with theories suggesting heightened genetic influences on educational attainment during periods of greater social and educational opportunity.

However, four key gaps in knowledge remain. **First**, a visual inspection of Figure 1 indicates potential differences in education-genetic associations before reunification, particularly for individuals who attended school between 1945 and 1965 (i.e., birth years 1930 to 1950). During this period, the Soviet-influenced East German government dismantled the traditional tripartite school system, replacing it with a ten-year comprehensive model aimed at reducing class-based inequalities and promoting socialist values. **Second**, while genomic predictors of education are associated with intergenerational educational mobility — defined as being more or less educated than one's parents in cohort-relative terms (Belsky et al., 2018) — it is unknown to what extent these associations may vary across Germany's historically changing opportunity structures. **Third**, sociological and economic studies suggest that both genetic associations with educational attainment and intergenerational mobility may vary by gender (Herd et al., 2019). **Fourth**, the distinct subcomponents of the PGI-Education — such as variants associated with cognitive versus non-cognitive skills — that might differentially predict educational attainment and intergenerational mobility over time remain unclear. To address these gaps, a larger German genetic dataset is needed to perform analyses with sufficient statistical power.

This study aims to elucidate whether and when genetic associations with education have remained stable, attenuated, or amplified over historical, social, and political transformations in Germany and whether these associations extend to intergenerational educational mobility. We outline an analysis plan that harmonizes genomic, educational, and regional data from four German cohorts, encompassing over 15,000 individuals born between 1920 and 2000. Given the prior research in SOEP-G and TwinLife cohorts (Fraemke et al., 2025; Spörlein et al., 2025), this analysis plan should not be considered a strict preregistration, because parts of the data have already been analysed and inspected and some hypotheses are informed by these earlier findings. Nevertheless, publishing an analysis plan for data harmonized and synthesized across all four cohorts remains valuable, as it clarifies the analytic approach, prevents (re)formulating hypotheses after the results are known, and helps mitigate the risk of false positives.

Our mega-analysis probes whether the association of DNA-based educational biomarkers with educational attainment and intergenerational educational mobility varies by age cohort, region and gender. We include four education-genomic indicators: PGI-Education (EA4; Okbay et al., 2022), which can be further split into PGIs for cognitive and non-cognitive skills (Malanchini et al., 2024), and within-family PGI-Education based on a within-family GWAS (Tan et al., 2024). In addition, we will compute the total SNP-based heritability estimates for educational attainment in East and West Germany before and after reunification. We designate **EA4 as the primary indicator** and the other metrics as secondary measures for further exploration. We will control for genetic ancestry using Germany-specific ancestry estimates (Harerimana et al., in preparation).

If we detect significant interactions between the genetic predictors and the environment (defined as region, cohort and gender), we will conduct additional sensitivity analyses, including a negative control analysis using a PGI for Height (Yengo et al., 2022), an investigation of variance dispersion in the outcome, and a covariate control for post-reunification intra-German migration. A larger exploration of genetics and internal migration behaviors is currently underway (Harerimana et al., in preparation).

## Methods

### Participants

> **Figure 2.** Overview of cohorts (A) and sample sizes (B) in the present study.

The analyses in this study will be based on the two Germany-wide cohorts, SOEP-G and TwinLife, as well as the two regional cohorts, BASE-II and SHIP, which will result in a total of N ≈ 15,000 participants. We included all individuals born between 1920 and 2000, **excluding those younger than 25 years at data collection**, who may not have completed their education (OECD, 2024).

All education-genomic analyses will be restricted to participants who are highly similar in genetic ancestry to European reference groups, which formed the basis of the GWAS discovery samples, to avoid the risk of confounding due to population stratification (Aikins et al., 2024; Price et al., 2006).

All measures in the four cohorts will be harmonized as reported in Table 1 and analyses will be conducted in a mega-analysis (cf. Tropf et al., 2017). This includes a harmonization of DNA preprocessing, Germany-specific ancestry estimates, and polygenic index computations (Harerimana et al., in preparation).

#### SOEP / SOEP-G

The Socio-Economic Panel (SOEP) is a population-based, multigenerational survey study (Goebel et al., 2019). 6,576 SOEP participants were randomly selected and invited to participate in buccal DNA genotyping as part of the SOEP-Gene subsample (SOEP-G; Koellinger et al., 2023). In total, education-genomic data are available for **N = 2,262 adults** (M_age = 56.13, SD_age = 18.72, 54 % female), with 98 % of participants showing high genetic similarity to European reference groups (see Koellinger et al., 2023). Genotyping was conducted using the Illumina Infinium Global Screening Array-24 v3.0 BeadChips.

#### TwinLife

The German Twin Family Panel (TwinLife) is a representative, cross-sequential longitudinal study (Mönkediek et al., 2019). The genome-wide genotyping in TwinLife is ongoing. As of now, genotyping was performed for a subset of n = 5,881 TwinLife participants. This results in a genome-wide genotype and PGI dataset for n = 5,432 individuals (i.e., twins, their siblings and parents) after quality control (55 % female). Genotyping was performed using Infinium Global Screening Arrays customized to include additional marker content of relevance to psychiatric disorders. Applying the above-described selection criteria, i.e. excluding individuals younger than 25 years at data collection, who may not have completed their education, reduced the sample to **2,957 individuals**.

#### BASE-II

The Berlin Aging Study II (BASE-II) is a multidisciplinary and multi-institutional project that ascertains a large number of ageing-related variables from a wide range of different functional domains. BASE-II is linked to the SOEP variables and comprises genetic data, which makes it a suitable cohort for this study. Genotyping was performed for **n = 1,600 older adults** (age range 60–80 years), as well as **n = 600 younger adults** (20–35 years), using the Genome-Wide Human SNP Array 6.0 from Affymetrix Inc. (50 % female).

#### SHIP

Study of Health in Pomerania (SHIP) includes the SHIP-START and the SHIP-TREND population-based cohorts (age range 20–79 years) to investigate health and mortality in the East German region of Mecklenburg/West Pomerania (Völzke et al., 2022). Whole genome DNA data were collected for **N = 4,081 in SHIP-START** across three waves between 1997 and 2012 (SHIP-0, SHIP-1 and SHIP-2) as well as for **N = 986 (SHIP-TREND)** between 2008 and 2011. SHIP-START was genotyped using the Human SNP 6.0 Array (Affymetrix, Santa Clara, CA, USA). SHIP-TREND participants included in the present analyses were genotyped using the Illumina HumanOmni2.5-Quad array.

Parents' years of education will be computed in accordance with the individual's educational attainment. Tertiary education in years will be added to the years spent in secondary education (e.g., +1.5 years for vocational training or +5 years for a university degree). For each respondent *i*, we will z-standardize years of education within birth cohort (subtracting the cohort-specific mean and dividing by the cohort-specific standard deviation) to obtain a child education score `ChildEdu_z,i`. For each parent, we will analogously z-standardize years of education within the parent's birth cohort and then average the two standardized scores to obtain a parental education score `ParEdu_z,i`. ### Note on coding (adjustment from the original plan)

An earlier draft of this analysis plan coded Region and Gender as 0/1 dummy variables, with West and female set as the reference categories. In the present revision, both predictors are contrast-coded:

- Region: `West = −0.5`, `East = +0.5`
- Gender: `female = +0.5`, `male = −0.5`

This change does not affect the model fits, predictions, or *p*-values for the focal three- and four-way interactions (β_14 in Equation 1; β_21 in Equation 4; β_15 and γ_15 in Equations 6–7; the focal tests of the spline and logistic-transition specifications). It changes the interpretation of the lower-order coefficients and the intercept.

Three considerations motivate the contrast-coded specification. First, lower-order coefficients then represent average effects across categories rather than simple effects evaluated at the reference category. In the four-way model of Analysis C1, for example, β_14 (PGI × BY × R) under 0/1 dummy coding represents the focal three-way effect among women specifically, whereas under contrast coding it represents the same effect averaged across genders. The latter is the conventional reading of "main effects" in factorial designs. Second, contrast coding reduces multicollinearity between lower-order and higher-order interaction terms in models that include several interactions, improving estimation stability. Third, the intercept β_0 represents the expected outcome at the unweighted midpoint of group means rather than at the reference group, which is the more even-handed baseline for a plan whose central aim is to test regional, cohort, and gender variation.

## Statistical Analyses

We conduct three sets of analyses. **First**, we test whether associations between PGI-Education and years of education vary by birth cohort and region. **Second**, we examine whether associations between PGI-Education and relative intergenerational educational mobility vary by birth cohort and region. **Third**, we assess whether these education-genetic associations differ by gender. All analyses will account for Germany-specific ancestry principal components and technical artifacts by residualizing each PGI prior to analysis. The alpha level will be set to **α = .05** for all tests.

### Power Analysis

Prior to conducting the main analyses and to contextualize potential null effects, a systematic power analysis will be performed using **Monte Carlo simulations**. In this procedure, educational attainment will be randomly permuted and various underlying true effect sizes, including a null effect, will be simulated. Using a pre-coded script, this procedure ensures that the resulting power estimates reflect the actual data properties, such as the observed standard deviations, variable distributions and the defined function.

### A. Whether education-genetic associations vary by age cohort and region

#### Analysis A1 — linear birth-year × region interaction

We first examine whether our genetic (PGI) × age cohort (BY) × region (R) interaction found in SOEP-G (Fraemke et al., 2025) is present across all four cohorts. We enter time as a continuous variable and estimate the following model in **Equation (1)** (cf. Fraemke et al., 2025):

```
Y_i = β_0
    + β_1·PGI_i
    + β_2·BY_i
    + β_3·R_i
    + β_4·(PGI_i × BY_i)
    + β_5·(PGI_i × R_i)
    + β_6·(BY_i × R_i)
    + β_7·Gen_i
    + β_8·(R_i × Gen_i)
    + β_9·(PGI_i × Gen_i)
    + β_10·(BY_i × Gen_i)
    + β_11·(BY_i × R_i × Gen_i)
    + β_12·(PGI_i × BY_i × Gen_i)
    + β_13·(PGI_i × R_i × Gen_i)
    + β_14·(PGI_i × BY_i × R_i)                           ← FOCAL
    + ε_i,                                  ε_i ~ N(0, σ²)
```

**Hypotheses (focal):**

- H₀: β_14 = 0
- H₁: β_14 ≠ 0

where Y_i denotes years of education for individual *i*, PGI_i is z-standardized PGI-Education, BY_i is the centered birth year, R_i is a contrast-coded indicator for region (`−0.5 = West, +0.5 = East`), Gen_i is a contrast-coded indicator for self-reported gender (`+0.5 = female, −0.5 = male`), β_0 is the intercept representing the expected years of education at the unweighted gender mean and the unweighted region mean, in the average birth year, with average PGI, and ε_i is the individual-level error term, assumed normally distributed with constant variance. **All continuous variables, including covariates, will be z-standardized.**

This specification allows us to test whether the PGI–Education association varies as a function of both birth year and region, while controlling for main and lower-order interaction effects with gender.

#### Analysis A2 — step-function reunification × region interaction

In the second step, we replace Birth Year (BY) in Equation (1) with a dichotomous **Reunification** variable, coded `0` for individuals who turned 15 before 1990 and `1` for those who turned 15 in or after 1990. The model specification remains otherwise identical to A1, with all main effects, lower-order interaction terms, and gender covariates included. This substitution changes the focal interaction from `(PGI_i × BY_i × R_i)` to `(PGI_i × Reunification_i × R_i)`, thereby specifying the moderating role of birth as a step function rather than linear, testing whether the magnitude of PGI–Education associations differs between East and West Germany before versus after reunification.

#### Analysis A3 — non-linear cohort variation via GAMs

We extend our previous analyses and probe for non-linear changes in PGI-Education associations that may coincide with the historical shifts in the East German education system (e.g. the post-war reconstruction phase 1945–1965, the consolidation phase 1965–1989, and the post-reunification period 1990–today). To capture such potential non-linearities without imposing a specific functional form, we will fit varying-coefficient models (Generalized Additive Models; GAMs) in which the PGI–Education slope is allowed to vary flexibly across birth cohorts and to differ between East and West Germany (Wood, 2017, 2025). This will be implemented as **Equation (2)**:

```
Y_i = β_0 + β_1·PGI_i + β_2·BY_i + β_3·R_i + β_4·Gen_i
    + PGI_i × s(BY_i, k, R_i)
    + ε_i,                                  ε_i ~ N(0, σ²)
```

where `s(BY_i, k)` denotes a penalized regression spline basis (thin-plate by default, cubic splines as sensitivity analyses), and `k` is the basis dimension ('knots', set to 6–8 and checked for adequacy via the k-index). Model selection will be based on likelihood-ratio tests and information criteria (AIC/BIC). Significance will be evaluated at three levels:

(a) an **omnibus comparison** of models with and without cohort-varying terms;
(b) **approximate F-tests** for the region-specific effects, indicating whether PGI associations differ significantly across cohorts within each region;
(c) **planned contrasts** comparing East and West Germany based on simultaneous confidence intervals (Wood, 2017).

This approach will allow us to detect non-linear regional differences in PGI–Education associations, for example across the three suggested time phases. The full formulated hypotheses are reported in **Appendix 1** and a simulation is reported in **Appendix 2**.

### Sensitivity analyses (run only if A1–A3 yields significant gene–environment interaction parameters, e.g. β_14 in A1)

#### S1 — Replace primary predictor with secondary PGIs

We will re-estimate Analyses A1–A3 substituting the primary predictor (PGI-Education) with the secondary predictors **PGI-Cognitive**, **PGI-Non-Cognitive**, and **wf-PGI-Education**. Because these PGIs typically yield smaller effects, we anticipate reduced power to detect gene–environment interactions. We designate the main gene–environment interaction parameters (e.g., β_14 in A1) as the primary estimands and define **one multiplicity family** comprising the four tests of these coefficients across the (correlated) predictors — PGI-Education, PGI-Cognition, PGI-Non-Cognitive and wf-PGI-Education. This yields a family of four tests per analysis, and we will control multiplicity using the **Benjamini–Hochberg procedure at q = .05**.

#### S2 — Negative control with PGI-Height

We will perform a Negative Control Analysis with PGI-Height to assess whether interaction effects are unique to the educational domain or indicate a more general pattern of varying genetic associations over time. For this purpose, we will use the same equation substituting PGI-Education with PGI-Height and probing its association with both years of education and phenotypic Height.

#### S3 — Heteroscedasticity / variance dispersion

We will apply a Heteroscedasticity method to model the dispersion of the outcome and test whether a gene–environment interaction is specific to a measured predictor or represents a general pattern of variation in the outcome (Domingue et al., 2022). We will estimate an environmental and a genetic heteroscedasticity model for East and West Germany separately. If the spline approach yields significant smooth functions, we will conduct a **variance-aware GAM**.

#### S4 — Migration-composition diagnostic

We will conduct a sensitivity analysis examining whether regional differences in PGI-Education associations are statistically accounted for by East–West migration behavior. This will allow us to examine whether an apparent gene–environment interaction may be driven by migration-induced selection after reunification (Abdellaoui et al., 2019; Kuznetsov et al., 2023). This may be the case, because educational allocation in Germany is determined relative to peers within the same region and cohort (e.g., numerus clausus, track quotas). Therefore, what matters for academic placement is a student's **local standing** on PGI-Education — their percentile or z-score within region × cohort — rather than the absolute PGI value. If post-1989 migration lowered and narrowed the East German PGI distribution, the same absolute PGI would correspond to a higher local rank in the East than in the West. Under rank-based thresholds, this can mechanically steepen the observed PGI–Education association in the East without implying true contextual moderation or G × E.

> **Figure 3.** Simulated data of an extreme selective intra-German migration scenario.
>
> Under a scenario where all individuals with PGI-Education > +1 SD leave East Germany, the East PGI distribution is truncated and narrowed relative to the West (lower mean, smaller variance). Because academic placement is referenced within cohort and region, the upper educational tracks (e.g., University) in the East are then filled by the next-highest PGI individuals. Under this range restriction of PGI but a within-region self-referencing of educational attainment, the observed PGI–Education slope in the East is steeper (β = 0.72 vs. β = 0.6).

We evaluate **three necessary (but not sufficient)** statistical implications of a migration-composition explanation: (1) the East has a lower mean and (2) lower variance of PGI than the West; and (3) the variance of years of education does not differ to a practically important degree between regions. Concretely:

**Test 1 — mean differences in PGI-Education (Welch two-sample t-test):**
- H₀: Mean(PGI)_East ≥ Mean(PGI)_West
- H₁: Mean(PGI)_East < Mean(PGI)_West

**Test 2 — variance differences in PGI-Education (F-test):**
- H₀: Var(PGI)_East > Var(PGI)_West
- H₁: Var(PGI)_East < Var(PGI)_West

**Test 3 — variance differences in years of education (F-test):**
- H₀: Var(Years of Education)_East < Var(Years of Education)_West
- H₁: Var(Years of Education)_East = Var(Years of Education)_West

Next, we re-estimate Region × PGI models replacing absolute PGI within region by a **standardized PGI** to align the predictor with the locally relative educational allocation and to diagnose whether the Region × PGI term reflects migration-driven differences in relative standing rather than environmental moderation. If the Region × PGI interaction **attenuates** after within-region standardization we will interpret the original interaction as consistent with migration-induced composition, but not as proof of causality. If the interaction **remains** of similar magnitude and statistically different from zero when using standardized PGI, we will infer that selective migration does not account for the East–West difference.

Prior research indicates that post-reunification East-to-West migration was gendered and education-selective — emigrants were disproportionately young women, and out-migration was positively selected on education (Kröhnert & Vollmer, 2012) — so we will explicitly test whether our migration-composition pattern is driven by gender by stratifying the diagnostics and adding **Gender × Region** terms in migration models.

#### S5 — SNP-heritability by Region × Time (GREML)

In addition, we will estimate the total population-level share of variance in education attributable to common autosomal SNPs within each region and time period. We will estimate **SNP-heritability (h²_SNP)** separately by Region (East vs. West) and Time (pre vs. post-reunification) using a multi-group genome-wide complex trait analysis (GCTA; Yang et al., 2011). The method builds a genome-wide genetic relatedness matrix from SNP data among individuals who are filtered to be approximately unrelated (pairwise genetic relatedness < 0.025) and applies restricted maximum likelihood in a linear mixed model to partition phenotypic variance into an additive SNP component and a residual, yielding h²_SNP. Analyses will use a harmonized intersecting autosomal SNP set across groups (imputation INFO ≥ .90, MAF ≥ .01, HWE p > 1×10⁻⁴). Once the exact sample size is known, we will conduct a power analysis using the **GCTA-GREML Power calculator** (Visscher et al., 2014). We will not further stratify for analyses B and C into smaller subgroups, as doing so would substantially reduce power for h²_SNP estimation. To evaluate whether SNP-heritability differs across Region × Time strata, we will compare nested multi-group GREML models using **likelihood-ratio tests**.

- H₀: h²_SNP, East, pre = h²_SNP, East, post = h²_SNP, West, pre = h²_SNP, West, post
- H₁: not all group-specific h²_SNP are equal.

Operationally, we will implement this by fitting multi-component GREML models with separate genetic variance components for each Region × Time group. In the full model, four group-specific genetic variance components are estimated (e.g.; σ²_G, "East,pre"), along with a residual variance term (e.g.; σ²_E, "East,pre"). The nested models will impose equality constraints by combining the corresponding group-specific GRMs (for example, a null model with a single genetic variance component for all four groups) and thus assume equal additive genetic variance across the constrained groups. We then compare the full and nested models using twice the difference in REML log-likelihood, which is approximately chi-square distributed with degrees of freedom equal to the number of constraints on the genetic variance components.

In addition to h²_SNP, we will report the implied additive SNP variance `V_G = h²_SNP × V_P` (with V_P the residual phenotypic variance on the observed scale) for each Region × Time stratum. Comparing V_G across strata will clarify whether differences in h²_SNP reflect changes in genetic variance versus changes in phenotypic variance.

### B. Whether education-genetic associations with intergenerational educational mobility vary by age cohort and region

In a second set of analyses, we examine whether the association between PGI-Education and **relative intergenerational educational mobility** varies by birth cohort and region. Relative intergenerational mobility is operationalized as the difference between the respondent's cohort-standardized years of education and the average of the parents' cohort-standardized years of education (cf. Belsky et al., 2018). The mobility outcome is then defined as **Equation (3)**:

```
Mob_i = ChildEdu_z,i − ParEdu_z,i
```

All models will be restricted to individuals with non-missing information on own and parental education and will include Gender and, in line with Belsky et al. (2018), parents' cohort-standardized years of education, as a **covariate (not as a moderator)**. As in Analyses A1–A3, all continuous predictors, including covariates, will be z-standardized.

#### Analysis B1 — linear PGI × BY × Region interaction on mobility

We set the mobility outcome `Mob_i` as the dependent variable and estimate **Equation (4)**:

```
Mob_i = β_0
      + β_1·PGI_i
      + β_2·BY_i
      + β_3·R_i
      + β_4·(PGI_i × BY_i)
      + β_5·(PGI_i × R_i)
      + β_6·(BY_i × R_i)
      + β_7·Gen_i
      + β_8·(R_i × Gen_i)
      + β_9·(PGI_i × Gen_i)
      + β_10·(BY_i × Gen_i)
      + β_11·(BY_i × R_i × Gen_i)
      + β_12·(PGI_i × BY_i × Gen_i)
      + β_13·(PGI_i × R_i × Gen_i)
      + β_14·ParEdu_z,i
      + β_15·(R_i × ParEdu_z,i)
      + β_16·(PGI_i × ParEdu_z,i)
      + β_17·(BY_i × ParEdu_z,i)
      + β_18·(BY_i × R_i × ParEdu_z,i)
      + β_19·(PGI_i × BY_i × ParEdu_z,i)
      + β_20·(PGI_i × R_i × ParEdu_z,i)
      + β_21·(PGI_i × BY_i × R_i)                          ← FOCAL
      + ε_i,                                  ε_i ~ N(0, σ²)
```

Here, Mob_i denotes relative intergenerational mobility for individual *i*, PGI_i is z-standardized PGI-Education, BY_i is centered birth year, R_i is a contrast-coded indicator for region (`−0.5 = West, +0.5 = East`), and Gen_i is a contrast-coded indicator for self-reported gender (`+0.5 = female, −0.5 = male`). The intercept β_0 represents expected mobility at the unweighted gender mean and the unweighted region mean, in the average birth year, with average PGI. The focal parameter is the three-way interaction **β_21**:

- H₀: β_21 = 0
- H₁: β_21 ≠ 0

This specification allows us to test whether PGI–mobility associations vary jointly with birth cohort and region, while controlling for main effects and lower-order interactions.

#### Analysis B2 — step-function Reunification × Region on mobility

In the second step, we replace Birth Year (BY) in the mobility model with a dichotomous **Reunification** variable (P), coded `0` for individuals who turned 15 before 1990 and `1` for those who turned 15 in or after 1990. The model specification remains otherwise identical to B1, with the mobility outcome Mob_i as dependent variable and including PGI-Education, region, gender, and parents' cohort-standardized years of education (ParEdu_z,i) as covariates, together with all corresponding main effects and lower-order interaction terms. This substitution changes the focal interaction from `(PGI_i × BY_i × R_i)` to `(PGI_i × Reunification_i × R_i)`, thereby specifying the moderating role of historical time as a step function rather than a linear trend and testing whether the magnitude of PGI–mobility associations differs between East and West Germany before versus after reunification.

#### Analysis B3 — non-linear PGI × BY × Region on mobility via GAMs

Third, we will probe for potential non-linear changes in PGI–mobility associations across birth cohorts that may coincide with major institutional shifts in the East German and West German education systems. We will fit varying-coefficient generalized additive models (GAMs) in which the PGI–mobility slope is allowed to vary smoothly across birth cohorts and to differ between East and West Germany — **Equation (5)**:

```
Mob_i = β_0 + β_1·PGI_i + β_2·BY_i + β_3·R_i + β_4·Gen_i + β_5·ParEdu_z,i
      + PGI_i × s(BY_i, k, R_i)
      + ε_i,                                  ε_i ~ N(0, σ²)
```

Here, `s(BY_i, k)` denotes a penalized regression spline basis for birth year (thin-plate splines by default, with cubic splines as a sensitivity analysis), and `k` is the basis dimension (set to 6–8 and checked via the k-index). The spline basis is allowed to differ by region (East vs. West). Model selection will rely on likelihood-ratio tests and information criteria (AIC/BIC). Significance will be evaluated at three levels:

(a) an omnibus comparison of models with and without cohort-varying terms;
(b) approximate F-tests for the region-specific smooths, indicating whether PGI–mobility associations vary across cohorts within each region;
(c) planned contrasts comparing East and West Germany based on simultaneous confidence intervals for the difference between smooths.

This approach will allow us to detect non-linear regional differences in PGI–mobility associations across historical time.

If one of the analysis strategies reveals significant parameters (β_21 in B1; β_15-related terms in B2; or the spline model in B3), **sensitivity analyses S1–S3 will be repeated here**. If the spline or logistic approach yields significant results, we will test for heteroscedasticity, using a variance-aware logistic or spline approach.

Taken together, Analyses A1–A3 and B1–B3 characterize how PGI-Education relates to both absolute educational attainment and relative intergenerational mobility across cohorts and regions, while adjusting for gender and (for mobility) parental educational background. In a final step, we examine whether these temporal and regional patterns differ between women and men.

### C. Whether education-genetic associations differ by gender

In this final set of analyses, we ask whether the cohort- and region-specific patterns documented in Analyses A and B differ for women and men. To do so, we extend the linear, step-function, and spline specifications previously applied to educational attainment (Y_i) and relative mobility (Mob_i) by including gender as an explicit moderator of the PGI × cohort × region effects. Throughout, gender remains contrast-coded as `Gen_i ∈ {+0.5, −0.5}` (female = +0.5, male = −0.5), and all continuous covariates (including parental education in the mobility models) are z-standardized as before.

#### Analysis C1 — four-way PGI × BY × R × Gender interaction (linear)

For educational attainment, we extend Equation (1) by adding the four-way interaction term `β_15·(PGI_i × BY_i × R_i × Gen_i)` as the focal parameter, retaining all main effects and lower-order interactions already specified in A1 — **Equation (6)**:

```
Y_i = [Equation (1)] + β_15·(PGI_i × BY_i × R_i × Gen_i) + ε_i,
      ε_i ~ N(0, σ²).
```

For mobility, we apply the same extension to Equation (4), with Mob_i as the dependent variable and ParEdu_z,i included as a covariate, as in B1 — **Equation (7)**:

```
Mob_i = [Equation (4)] + γ_15·(PGI_i × BY_i × R_i × Gen_i) + ε_i,
        ε_i ~ N(0, σ²).
```

**Focal hypotheses:**
- H₀: β_15 = 0 and γ_15 = 0
- H₁: β_15 ≠ 0 and/or γ_15 ≠ 0

testing whether the PGI × Birth Year × Region interaction differs between women and men for educational attainment and mobility, respectively.

#### Analysis C2 — gender-specific logistic transition across birth years

We will model the PGI–education association as a **logistic transition** across birth years. This may capture East–West gender differences arising only in certain time periods. Following our previous SOEP-G analyses (Fraemke et al., 2025), we will assume a general positive linear trend component (`δ·BY`) as baseline and a lower (L) and an upper bound (U) that will be set globally across the whole sample. We will then define gender-specific **location** of the logistic curve (`κ_Gen`) as well as its **steepness** (`τ_Gen`).

We specify **Equation (8)**:

```
Y_i = α_0 + α_1·BY_i + α_2·Gen_i + PGI_i · f(BY_i, Gen_i) + ε_i,
      ε_i ~ N(0, σ²)
```

with the cohort-varying PGI slope given by **Equation (9)**:

```
f(BY_i, Gen_i) = δ·BY_i + L + (U − L) · logit⁻¹( κ_Gen · [BY_i − τ_0,Gen] )
```

where L and U are global lower/upper bounds of the PGI slope; `δ·BY_i` is an additional linear trend; `τ_0,Gen` is the **location** and `κ_Gen` the **steepness**, both allowed to differ by gender (Gen ∈ {0, 1}). Interpretation: the PGI effect on education transitions from L to U over cohorts, with gender-specific timing and steepness.

Model fit for this logistic specification will be compared to the previously suggested linear and a categorical alternative using likelihood-ratio tests (LRT) and information criteria (AIC/BIC). The linear alternative is the special case U = L, where the logistic term vanishes and `f(BY_i, Gen_i) = δ·BY_i + L`.

- H₀: U = L (no logistic transition; PGI effect changes linearly with BY)
- H₁: U > L (with gender-specific τ_0,Gen and κ_Gen)

> **Note:** Since U = L places a parameter on the boundary we will use an LRT with a 50:50 chi-square mixture and compare AIC/BIC as usual (Self & Liang, 1987).

If the logistic model is preferred, we will test for a **Region × Gender** interaction on the location and steepness of the logistic curve, with `R_i ∈ {−0.5 = West, +0.5 = East}` and `Gen_i ∈ {+0.5 = female, −0.5 = male}`, keeping global U, L and δ, and decomposing location and steepness additively into:

```
τ_0,Gen = τ + τ_S·Gen_i + τ_R·R + τ_RS·(Gen_i × R)
κ_Gen   = κ + κ_S·Gen_i + κ_R·R + κ_RS·(Gen_i × R)
```

and **Equation (10)**:

```
f(BY_i, Gen_i) = δ·BY_i + L + (U − L) · logit⁻¹( κ_RS · [BY_i − τ_0,RS] )
```

and test:
- H₀: τ_RS = 0, κ_RS = 0 (no Region × Gender differences in timing or steepness)
- H₁: τ_RS ≠ 0 and/or κ_RS ≠ 0

> **Figure 4.** Hypothetical gender-specific logistic transition of the PGI–education association across birth cohorts in East and West Germany.
>
> Illustrates a hypothetical logistic functional form of the genotype–phenotype relationship for East and West German women, where τ_0,RS > 0 but κ_RS = 0. Men are collapsed across regions and shown only as a reference to reduce visual complexity.

This analysis will be implemented for educational attainment as the outcome; the same logistic structure can be applied analogously to the mobility outcome if warranted by sample size and model complexity.

#### Analysis C3 — gender-stratified non-linear cohort profiles via GAMs

Building on A3 and B3, we fit varying-coefficient GAMs in which the PGI slope is modeled as a smooth function of Birth Year, now stratified by **Region × Gender** group. For educational attainment, this extends Equation (2) to:

```
Y_i = β_0 + β_2·PGI_i + β_3·BY_i + β_4·R_i + β_5·Gen_i
    + PGI_i × s(BY_i, k; R_i, Gen_i)
    + ε_i,
```

where the smooth `s(·)` is estimated separately for each Region × Gender combination (West–female, West–male, East–female, East–male). For mobility, we use the analogous specification with Mob_i as outcome and ParEdu_z,i included as a linear covariate, as in B3. As in A3/B3, we evaluate:

(a) omnibus tests comparing models with and without cohort-stratified splines;
(b) approximate F-tests for each Region × Gender smooth;
(c) planned contrasts of the difference between smooths across Region × Gender groups using simultaneous confidence intervals.

If one of the analysis strategies reveals significant parameters (β_15 or γ_15 in C1; τ_0,RS or κ_RS in C2; or the spline model in C3), **sensitivity analyses S1–S3 will be repeated here**. If the spline or logistic approach yields significant results, we will test for heteroscedasticity using a variance-aware logistic or spline approach.

## Appendix 1 — Hypothesis tests for the spline models

In Analyses A1 and C (Analysis 2, sic — refers to A3 / C3) we propose spline-based varying-coefficient models to examine whether the association between PGI–Education and education varies nonlinearly across birth cohorts and differs by Region or Region × Gender groups. To ensure clarity and reproducibility, we here provide the explicit null and alternative hypotheses corresponding to the three levels of inference in the spline framework. These tests formalize how we will evaluate (a) the overall need for cohort-varying PGI effects, (b) the presence of such effects within each group, and (c) differences between groups across the cohort dimension. For comprehensiveness we report the analysis with gender; for Analysis A1 the gender distinction will be omitted correspondingly.

**Equation (S1)** — full spline model with all four Region × Sex groups:

```
Y_i = β_0 + β_2·PGI_i + β_3·BY_i + β_4·R_i + β_5·Sex_i
    + s_0(BY_i, k) + PGI_i × s_G(BY_i, k)
    + ε_i,                                  ε_i ~ N(0, σ²)
```

where G ∈ {West–Men, West–Women, East–Men, East–Women} and each `s_G(·)` is a group-specific smooth of birth year.

### (a) Omnibus: "do we need cohort-varying PGI effects?"

**Test:** Compare the model with all `PGI_i × s(BY_i, k) × R_i × Sex_i` terms to a reduced model without these smooths, optionally retaining a linear `PGI_i × BY_i × R_i × Sex_i` term.

- H₀: s_G(BY_c) = 0 ∀ G (no cohort-dependent modulation of the PGI effect beyond the reduced model in any group)
- H₁: ∃ G: s_G(BY_c) ≠ 0 (cohort-dependent modulation of the PGI effect beyond the reduced model in at least one group)

**Inference:** Likelihood-ratio test on REML scores (approx. χ²) and AIC/BIC as corroboration.

### (b) Per-group smooth: "does the PGI effect vary over cohorts within group G?"

**Test:** approximate F-test for each smooth `s_G(·)`.

- H₀: s_G(BY_c) = 0 (PGI slope is constant over BY_c within group G)
- H₁: s_G(BY_c) ≠ 0 (or "not purely linear" in the deviation-from-linearity parameterization)

**Inference:** Report `edf`, `F`, and exact `p` for each G.

### (c) Planned group contrasts: "where do two groups differ over cohorts?"

**Test:** Difference-smooth with simultaneous confidence bands for `Δ_G1,G2(BY_c) = s_G1(BY_c) − s_G2(BY_c)`.

- H₀: Δ_G1,G2(BY_c) = 0 ∀ BY_c in the observed domain (no functional difference between the groups across cohorts)
- H₁: ∃ I ⊆ domain s.t. the 95 % simultaneous band for Δ_G1,G2(BY_c) excludes 0 on I (there are cohort intervals with reliably stronger/weaker PGI prediction in one group)

**Multiplicity:** We will control across multiple pairwise contrasts.

```r
set.seed(123)

N_per_region <- 8000
Region <- factor(rep(c("West","East"), each = N_per_region), levels = c("West","East"))
BY  <- runif(2*N_per_region, 1940, 2000)     # birth year (calendar)
BYc <- BY - 1970                              # centered birth year
PGI <- rnorm(2*N_per_region)                  # z-score

# West: linear cohort trend
beta_W_true <- 0.35 + 0.0025 * (BY - 1970)

# East: logistic cohort trend (bounds 0.1–0.6; location 1970; moderate steepness)
L <- 0.10; U <- 0.60; b <- 0.7
beta_E_true <- L + (U - L) / (1 + exp(-b * (BY - 1970)))

# Combine region-specific true effects
beta_true <- ifelse(Region == "West", beta_W_true, beta_E_true)

# Outcome with homoscedastic noise
mu <- 11.5 + 0.02*BYc + PGI * beta_true
sd <- 0.8 # increase if interested in more noisy analysis
set.seed(123)
y  <- rnorm(length(mu), mu, sd)

dat <- data.frame(y, BY, BYc, PGI, Region, beta_true)
```

### Extract β_R(BY) with pointwise SE

```r
extract_beta <- function(mod, region = c("West","East"),
                         n_grid = 400, BY_origin = 1970) {
  region <- match.arg(region)
  mf <- mod$model
  BYc_grid <- seq(min(mf$BYc), max(mf$BYc), length.out = n_grid)

  nd0 <- data.frame(BYc = BYc_grid, PGI = 0,
                    Region = factor(region, levels = levels(mf$Region)))
  nd1 <- nd0; nd1$PGI <- 1

  make_derived <- function(nd) {
    nd$PGI_W <- as.numeric(nd$Region == "West") * nd$PGI
    nd$PGI_E <- as.numeric(nd$Region == "East") * nd$PGI
    nd$by_W  <- nd$PGI_W
    nd$by_E  <- nd$PGI_E
    nd
  }
  nd0 <- make_derived(nd0)
  nd1 <- make_derived(nd1)

  X0 <- predict(mod, newdata = nd0, type = "lpmatrix", newdata.guaranteed = TRUE)
  X1 <- predict(mod, newdata = nd1, type = "lpmatrix", newdata.guaranteed = TRUE)

  D  <- X1 - X0
  b  <- coef(mod)
  V  <- vcov(mod)

  fit <- as.vector(D %*% b)
  se  <- sqrt(pmax(1e-12, rowSums((D %*% V) * D)))

  out <- data.frame(
    Region = region,
    BYc    = BYc_grid,
    BY     = BYc_grid + BY_origin,
    beta   = fit,
    se     = se
  )
  out[order(out$BY), ]
}

betas <- rbind(extract_beta(m1, "West"), extract_beta(m1, "East"))
```

### East − West difference with simultaneous 95 % CIs

```r
diff_beta_simul <- function(mod, n_grid = 300, sims = 4000, BY_origin = 1970, seed = 1) {
  mf <- mod$model
  BYc_grid <- seq(min(mf$BYc), max(mf$BYc), length.out = n_grid)

  make_nd <- function(region, PGI) {
    nd <- data.frame(BYc = BYc_grid, PGI = PGI,
                     Region = factor(region, levels = levels(mf$Region)))
    nd$PGI_W <- as.numeric(nd$Region == "West") * nd$PGI
    nd$PGI_E <- as.numeric(nd$Region == "East") * nd$PGI
    nd
  }

  X_E1 <- predict(mod, newdata = make_nd("East", 1), type = "lpmatrix", newdata.guaranteed = TRUE)
  X_E0 <- predict(mod, newdata = make_nd("East", 0), type = "lpmatrix", newdata.guaranteed = TRUE)
  X_W1 <- predict(mod, newdata = make_nd("West", 1), type = "lpmatrix", newdata.guaranteed = TRUE)
  X_W0 <- predict(mod, newdata = make_nd("West", 0), type = "lpmatrix", newdata.guaranteed = TRUE)

  D <- (X_E1 - X_E0) - (X_W1 - X_W0)

  b <- coef(mod); V <- vcov(mod)
  fit <- as.vector(D %*% b)
  se  <- sqrt(pmax(1e-12, rowSums((D %*% V) * D)))

  set.seed(seed)
  L <- try(chol(V), silent = TRUE)
  if (inherits(L, "try-error")) {
    Ee <- eigen((V + t(V))/2, symmetric = TRUE)
    L  <- t(Ee$vectors %*% diag(sqrt(pmax(Ee$values, 0))) %*% t(Ee$vectors))
  }
  Zmax <- replicate(sims, {
    z <- rnorm(ncol(V))
    f_draw <- as.vector(D %*% (b + L %*% z))
    max(abs((f_draw - fit) / se))
  })
  crit <- as.numeric(quantile(Zmax, 0.95, na.rm = TRUE))

  data.frame(BYc = BYc_grid, BY = BYc_grid + BY_origin,
             difference = fit, lower = fit - crit*se, upper = fit + crit*se)
}

d_E_minus_W <- diff_beta_simul(m1)

ggplot(d_E_minus_W, aes(BY, difference)) +
  geom_ribbon(aes(ymin = lower, ymax = upper), alpha = 0.2) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 0, linetype = 2) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 1) +
  labs(x = "Birth year", y = "East − West: β(PGI→Edu) difference",
       title = "Difference smooth with 95% simultaneous CI") +
  theme_minimal()
```
